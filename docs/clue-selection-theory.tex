\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Whenever we give a clue to a teammate with clue number $k$, they rank the unrevealed board words by how well each fits the clue word, and
choose words from the top, stopping when they reach $k$ words or when they select a word that is not ours, whichever comes first.

Suppose we and our teammate order words by cosine similarities from the same embedding space. Then, choosing the optimal clue is just a search over the clue
vocabulary for the word with the longest starting run of our team's words in the list sorted by similarity. In this case, our teammate chooses only the words that we intend, and never chooses a word that is not ours. This solution is trivial to find and is non-unique.

However, in practice, our perceived similarities are related to but different from our teammate's, which significantly changes the algorithm in which we find the optimal clue. In particular, this scenario is now probabilistic, whereas previously it was entirely deterministic, and we now need to deal with the possibility of our teammate choosing a neutral, opponent's, or the assassin word. This paper describes one algorithm in which we can find an optimal clue under these conditions. 

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Let $A$ be the set of our team's board words and $B$ the set of all other board words. For any fixed clue word, let
$a_i$ be its similarity to word $i \in A$ and $b_w$ its similarity to word
$w \in B$, where the $a_i$ are sorted so that $a_1 \ge a_2 \ge \dots$.
Selecting a word of $A$ is worth $1$, and selecting $w \in B$ costs
$c_w > 0$. We want to choose both parts of the clue.

We assume the guesser perceives word $x$'s similarity with the clue word as
$\hat z_x = z_x + \varepsilon_x$, where $z_x$ is our similarity to $x$ and the
$\varepsilon_x$ are independent $N(0, \sigma^2)$. This models differences
between our interpreted embeddings and the guesser's --- higher $\sigma$ means
a larger gap between the two. We also assume the guesser selects unrevealed
words in decreasing order of $\hat z$, stopping after $k$ selections or upon
selecting a word of $B$, whichever comes first.

In words, the guesser ranks the board the same way we do, perturbed by
independent noise of a single scale, and then guesses greedily. $\sigma$ is
the only free parameter.

\section{Reduction}
\label{sec:reduction}

Let
\[ D = \max_{w \in B} \hat z_w, \qquad W^\ast = \argmax_{w \in B} \hat z_w,
   \qquad N = \#\{\, i \in A : \hat z_i > D \,\}. \]
Every word ranked above $D$ is in $A$, since $D$ is the maximum of $\hat z$
over $B$. Thus the guesser's ordering begins with the $N$ words counted above,
and the $(N+1)$-th word is $W^\ast$. This means the guesser reveals
$\min(k, N)$ words of $A$, and selects a word of $B$ if and only if $N < k$,
in which case that word is $W^\ast$.

Note that no word of $B$ other than $W^\ast$ can be selected, so the single
random variable $D$ accounts for all of $B$. Write $\varphi$ and $\Phi$ for
the density and the distribution function of a standard normal. Each
$\hat z_w$ is $N(b_w, \sigma^2)$, so
\[ F_{\hat z_w}(x) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x - b_w}{\sigma}\right), \qquad
   f_{\hat z_w}(x) = \sigma^{-1}\varphi\!\left(\frac{x - b_w}{\sigma}\right). \]
Since $D$ is a maximum of independent variables,
\[ F_D(d) \;=\; \prod_{w \in B} F_{\hat z_w}(d)
   \;=\; \prod_{w \in B} \Phi\!\left(\frac{d - b_w}{\sigma}\right). \]

\section{Conditional distribution}
\label{sec:conditional}

$D$ is a function of $(\varepsilon_w)_{w \in B}$ only, which is independent of
$(\varepsilon_i)_{i \in A}$. Thus conditioning on $D = d$ does not change the
distribution of the $\hat z_i$, and each $i \in A$ independently satisfies
$\hat z_i > d$ with probability
\[ p_i(d) \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{a_i - d}{\sigma}\right). \]
Given $D = d$, the count $N$ is therefore a sum of $|A|$ independent Bernoulli
variables, the $i$-th with success probability $p_i(d)$.

\section{Objective}
\label{sec:objective}

From Section~\ref{sec:reduction} the guesser reveals $\min(k, N)$ words of $A$, and selects
$W^\ast$ exactly when $N < k$. Each word of $A$ is worth $1$ and $W^\ast$
costs $c_{W^\ast}$, so the value of announcing $k$ is $G(k) - P(k)$, where
\[ G(k) = \mathbb E\bigl[\min(k, N)\bigr], \qquad
   P(k) = \mathbb E\bigl[\, c_{W^\ast}\,\mathbf 1\{N < k\} \,\bigr]. \]
Since $\min(k, N) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \mathbf 1\{N \ge j\}$, taking expectations
and conditioning on $D$ gives
\[ G(k) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{k} \int \Pr(N \ge j \mid D = d) \,\, dF_D(d). \]
Each $\Pr(N \ge j \mid D = d)$ follows from the Bernoulli sum of
Section~\ref{sec:conditional}, and the integral is one-dimensional in $d$. Section~\ref{sec:computation} computes both. It
remains to find $P$.

In $P$ the cost $c_{W^\ast}$ and the event $\{N < k\}$ both depend on the
noise, but they become independent once $D$ is fixed. To see this, note that
$D$ is a function of $(\varepsilon_w)_{w \in B}$ alone, so conditioning on
$D = d$ constrains that family and leaves $(\varepsilon_i)_{i \in A}$ with its
original distribution. Given $D = d$, the count
$N = \#\{i \in A : z_i + \varepsilon_i > d\}$ is a function of
$(\varepsilon_i)_{i \in A}$, while the identity $W^\ast$ is a function of
$(\varepsilon_w)_{w \in B}$. The two families are independent, so $N$ and
$W^\ast$ are conditionally independent given $D$, and the expectation splits:
\[ P(k) \;=\; \int \Pr(N < k \mid D = d)\; \bar c(d) \,\, dF_D(d), \qquad
   \bar c(d) = \mathbb E\bigl[\, c_{W^\ast} \mid D = d \,\bigr]. \]
The first factor is again a probability for the Bernoulli sum of
Section~\ref{sec:conditional}.
It remains to compute $\bar c(d)$, for which we need
$\Pr(W^\ast = w \mid D = d)$.

Writing $g_w$ for the joint density of $W^\ast = w$ and $D$ at $d$,
\[ \Pr(W^\ast = w \mid D = d) \;=\; \frac{g_w(d)}{f_D(d)}. \]
$W^\ast = w$ and $D = d$ means $\hat z_w = d$ and $\hat z_v < d$ for every
other $v \in B$. The $\hat z$ are independent, so
\[ g_w(d) \;=\; f_{\hat z_w}(d) \prod_{v \ne w} F_{\hat z_v}(d). \]
The events $\{W^\ast = w\}$, $w \in B$, are disjoint and exhaustive, so
\[ f_D(d) \;=\; \sum_{w \in B} g_w(d). \]
From Section~\ref{sec:reduction}, $F_D(d) = \prod_{v \in B} F_{\hat z_v}(d)$, so the product
over $v \ne w$ is $F_D(d) \big/ F_{\hat z_w}(d)$. Set
\[ h_w(d) \;=\; \frac{f_{\hat z_w}(d)}{F_{\hat z_w}(d)}
   \;=\; \frac{\sigma^{-1}\varphi\bigl((d - b_w)/\sigma\bigr)}
              {\Phi\bigl((d - b_w)/\sigma\bigr)}, \]
so that $g_w(d) = F_D(d)\, h_w(d)$ and $f_D(d) = F_D(d) \sum_v h_v(d)$. The
$F_D(d)$ cancels in the ratio:
\[ \Pr(W^\ast = w \mid D = d) \;=\; \frac{h_w(d)}{\sum_v h_v(d)}, \qquad
   \bar c(d) \;=\; \frac{\sum_w c_w\, h_w(d)}{\sum_w h_w(d)}. \]
We choose the clue maximizing the value,
\[ \text{clue}^\ast \;=\; \argmax_{\text{word},\; 1 \le k \le K}
   \bigl[\, G(k) - P(k) \,\bigr]. \]

\section{Computation}
\label{sec:computation}

Both $G$ and $P$ have the form $\int \psi(d)\, dF_D(d)$ for a bounded $\psi$:
\[ \psi_G(d) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \Pr(N \ge j \mid D = d), \qquad
   \psi_P(d) = \Pr(N < k \mid D = d)\, \bar c(d). \]
Both are evaluated by numerical integration. Partition an interval
$[d_0, d_M]$ into cells $[d_m, d_{m+1}]$, take $\psi$ at the midpoint
$\bar d_m = \tfrac{1}{2}(d_m + d_{m+1})$ of each, and weight it by the
probability that $D$ lands in that cell:
\[ u_m \;=\; F_D(d_{m+1}) - F_D(d_m), \qquad
   \int \psi(d) \, dF_D(d) \;\approx\; \sum_m \psi(\bar d_m)\, u_m. \]
The $u_m$ are exact, being differences of the product in
Section~\ref{sec:reduction}, so the
only error is the variation of $\psi$ within a cell.

Take $d_0$ below every $b_w$ and $d_M$ above every $b_w$, each by several
$\sigma$, so that $F_D(d_0)$ and $1 - F_D(d_M)$ are negligible and no mass
falls outside the grid. The same grid and the same $u_m$ serve both integrals,
so one pass over the cells gives $G(k)$ and $P(k)$ together, and for every
$k \le K$ at once.

Within a cell, $\bar c(\bar d_m)$ is immediate from its definition, at a cost
of $|B|$ terms. The probabilities $\Pr(N \ge j \mid D = \bar d_m)$ follow from
the standard recursion for a sum of independent Bernoulli variables. Letting
$q^{(i)}_r$ be $\Pr(N = r)$ after the first $i$ words of $A$,
\[ q^{(0)}_0 = 1, \qquad
   q^{(i)}_r \;=\; q^{(i-1)}_r\bigl(1 - p_i(\bar d_m)\bigr)
                \;+\; q^{(i-1)}_{r-1}\,p_i(\bar d_m), \]
and $\Pr(N \ge j \mid D = \bar d_m) = 1 - \sum_{r < j} q_r$. Counts above $K$
are never used, so the recursion may be truncated there, costing $|A|K$ per
cell rather than $|A|^2$.

The total is $O\bigl(M(|A|K + |B|)\bigr)$ per clue, with $M$ the number of
cells. Every step is a fixed sequence of arithmetic on arrays of the same
shape, so the whole clue vocabulary is scored at once rather than one clue at
a time.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The model above has one free parameter. To choose it, $\sigma$ was swept over
three orders of magnitude and each value was made to play the same 100
positions, drawn from held-out board words and spanning openings through
endgames. Each turn was scored as the game scores it: the guesser's ranking is
walked, own words are counted until the first word of $B$, and the reward is
$+1$ per own word with $-0.2$, $-1$ and $-10$ for a neutral, opponent and
assassin word respectively. The guesser was an external language model, not
the similarity ranking the spymaster itself uses, so the numbers measure the
model against a listener it has no access to.

Both limits saturate. As $\sigma \to 0$ the model treats the guesser as a copy
of itself and announces $k = K$ on nearly every board, and as $\sigma$ grows
it announces $k = 1$ and stops responding to further increases. Neither end is
optimal, and the reward curve is single-peaked between them.

Note that the number of words revealed and the reward earned are maximized at
different $\sigma$. Revealed words peak at $\sigma = 1.0$ with $1.74$ per
turn, where reward is only $0.852$; reward peaks at $\sigma = 2.5$ with
$1.314$, where $1.41$ words are revealed. The difference is entirely risk. At
$\sigma = 1.0$ the guesser reaches a word of $B$ on 76 of 100 turns and the
assassin on 4; at $\sigma = 2.5$ it reaches $B$ on 20 and the assassin on
none. Thus the gain from claiming an extra word is real but is more than
repaid, and an objective fitted on revealed words alone would choose a value
that loses games.

The assassin is what makes the left half of the curve hard to measure. It is
worth $-10$, so a handful of assassin turns dominate the variance: the
standard error is $0.2$ to $0.3$ for $\sigma \le 2.0$ and $0.035$ to $0.069$
for $\sigma \ge 2.5$, where no assassin was reached at all. Comparing paired
by board, $\sigma = 2.5$ beats every other value tested at the 5\% level
except $\sigma = 2.0$ $(+0.14 \pm 0.21)$ and $\sigma = 1.0$
$(+0.46 \pm 0.49)$, both of which are indistinguishable only because their own
assassin losses are so noisy.

Note also that the gap between announced and revealed is negative everywhere
and closes monotonically, reaching $-0.11$ at $\sigma = 4$. The model
therefore remains optimistic at its own optimum, announcing $1.72$ where
$1.41$ is delivered. Calibrating $G$ and maximizing reward are different
objectives: only the ordering of $G(k) - P(k)$ across clues and $k$ affects
the choice made, so a bias shared by every candidate leaves the argmax
unchanged. Fitting $\sigma$ to close the gap would report $\sigma \approx 4$
and cost $0.25$ reward per turn.

We therefore take $\sigma = 2.5$. The positions used here are disjoint from
the evaluation boards, so the value was not chosen on the boards it is later
reported on.
