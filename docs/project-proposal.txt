CodenamesAI: A Non-LLM Clue Generator for Codenames

Shane Lee (shane.lee@yale.edu)
Project proposal


1. INTRODUCTION

Codenames is a popular word-association board game where two teams compete to guess secret agent words. One player per team, the spymaster, knows which words belong to their team, and must signal to their teammates by giving one-word clues, along with a number for how many words the clue is intended for.

Codenames has attracted a fair amount of work, including a recurring Codenames AI competition built around a shared agent framework, but much of it either studies simplified abstractions in which words are replaced by vectors, so that choosing a clue becomes a tractable optimization problem, or hands the clue-giving to a large language model and measures how well it plays.

My goal is to design a model that gives clues on the real game, intended for humans to interpret, and without a language model producing the clue at any point. My belief is that the best Codenames players do not rely entirely on their depth of language, or wordplay/trivia, but also a deep understanding of game theory and of how humans think. While non-LLM models may struggle with the former, the latter is where they can compete, since reasoning about the game and about the guesser can be written down explicitly and measured. I hope to develop models that incorporate such elements.



2. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

The closest work to mine comes out of one group at BYU. Bills, Archibald and
Blaylock's "Improving Cooperation in Language Games with Bayesian Inference and
the Cognitive Hierarchy" (AAAI 2025) separates two ways a partner can be wrong
about you: semantics, meaning which word embedding they use to decide two words
are related, and pragmatics, meaning what strategy they apply on top of it.
Their agent keeps a probability distribution over possible partners, each one an
embedding paired with a strategy, and updates it with Bayes' rule as it watches
the partner play. To make its otherwise deterministic model agents stochastic it
adds Gaussian noise to word embeddings, then picks the clue maximizing expected
utility, estimated by sampling each model guesser ten times with the clue word
perturbed.

That paper builds on a deductive hierarchy from earlier work by Bills and
Archibald. A level zero guesser looks only at the current clue and the cards
still face up, while a guesser above that keeps beliefs over every possible
assignment of the board and throws out the ones inconsistent with the clues it
has heard. Agents one level apart play well together and agents further apart
play badly, which is the brittleness the Bayesian version repairs.

Archibald and Brosnahan's "Adapting to Teammates in a Cooperative Language Game"
(arXiv:2403.00823) attacks the same mismatch from the other side, keeping an
ensemble of experts and treating the choice of which one speaks this turn as a
multi-armed bandit. That needs a score for how well a single turn went, which
neither of the field's usual metrics gives, since win rate and win time are only
known once a game is over and trade off against each other ambiguously. So they
enumerate the 36 possible outcomes of a Codenames turn and fit a linear function
over how often each occurs, trained so the gap between two teams' scores
predicts which one wins.

Both papers circle the problem I have to solve. A spymaster's similarity scores
are its own, the guesser's sense of which words go together is related but
different, and every number announced is a bet on how big that difference is.
Where they adapt to a partner across a game, I am trying to give a good clue to
a partner I never observe.


3. PROBLEM DESCRIPTION

I want to design a model that takes the current game state and returns a clue
word together with a clue number, meant for a human guesser.

The game state includes the board, meaning which words are still unrevealed and
which of those belong to my team, the opponent, the neutral pile and the
assassin, and potentially the history of what has already happened, which matters
in Codenames more than it does in most games. A clue given two turns ago whose
intended words were not all taken is still live information, and so is what the
opponent has revealed and how much of their board they have left.

The model outputs exactly one word and one number, and the word has to be legal
under the game's rules. Because the guesser is a person, I cannot optimize
against anything that only a machine listener would track.


4. PLANNED APPROACHES

To compare models I will use a Claude agent as the guesser, hoping that the way
it associates words is closer to a human's than a simulated guesser would be. I
have not settled on metrics yet, but a reasonable place to start is the average
number of team words revealed per clue along with how often a clue leads the
guesser to an opponent word or the assassin. Those two have to be read together,
since a model that announces large numbers will look good on the first and bad on
the second, and the whole difficulty of the game is in the trade between them.

The first model will be a baseline using a single embedding space and the current
board state only. Once it plays well enough to be worth measuring against, I will
freeze it and use it as the opponent for every model I build afterward, which
gives me one consistent metric in win rate against a fixed opponent. That beats
per-clue averages, since it prices every kind of mistake at what it actually
costs in a game rather than counting mistakes separately.

After that I want to push in two fairly different directions. On the
representation side, different embedding spaces are built from different sources
and carry different knowledge, so a clue that one space scores highly can be
invisible to another. Trying several spaces, and possibly learning which to trust
in which situation, is the obvious first extension, with deep learning over those
representations to follow once there is a training signal worth fitting.

The other direction is about what the current board state leaves out. I would
like to use Monte Carlo simulation to look ahead, rolling out plausible
continuations including the opponent's likely moves and picking the clue that
leads to a better distribution of future positions rather than the one that
scores best this turn. That matters most when my remaining words are entangled
with the opponent's, where revealing two words now can hand over a much easier
board. Clue history is a second case, and the deductive hierarchy has
already been over some of this ground, though from the guesser's side and by
logical elimination. I want the spymaster's version of it, and a softer one. If I
give a clue and my teammate misses one of the words I meant, they usually still
understood it and will take it on a later turn once something else clarifies it,
so what carries forward is not a deduced card identity but a word that has
already been most of the way communicated. Treating it that way, rather than as
a failure to retry from scratch, changes which clue is best next.

Past that the plan is mostly iteration: build something, measure it against the
frozen baseline, keep what works. One rule I intend to hold to throughout is that
Claude's guesses never go into any model as an input, at training time or
anywhere else. The evaluation guesser has to stay independent of whatever the
models are fitted on, or else I am tuning models to the exact listener I then
grade them against, and the numbers would be measuring overfitting instead of
skill.


5. DELIVERABLES

The main deliverable is a report covering each method I tried and how it
performed under the evaluation above, including the ones that did not work and
why. Along with it I will hand in the code for the whole pipeline, the frozen
baseline and every model built against it, and the recorded evaluation results
the report's comparisons come from.
