\section{Setup}

The goal is the same as in the proposal: a model that gives Codenames clues meant
for human guessers, without a language model producing the clue. Given the board
and which words belong to which team, the spymaster outputs one clue word and a
number.

To compare models we need a guesser, and we cannot have a person play hundreds of
games for every model, so the guesser is a language model. It only ever guesses.
It does not see the roles and does not write clues. Each turn it is given the
clue, the number and the unrevealed words, with the prompt

\begin{quote}\small
You are playing the guesser role in the board game Codenames. Your spymaster gave
the clue ``\emph{clue}'' for \emph{N} word(s). Here are the words still available to
guess: \emph{[words]}. Rank ALL of the words above by how likely you would guess
them for this clue, most likely first. Respond with ONLY a JSON array of the words
[\ldots]
\end{quote}

The turn then reveals the top $N$ words of its ranking in order and ends at the
first word that is not ours. So under this prompt the guesser always takes exactly
$N$ guesses unless it hits a wrong word first. At first the guesser was Claude
Sonnet 5. That cost a few dollars per 100 games, which limited how many
comparisons we could run, so we switched to gpt-oss-120b (open weights, served by
DeepInfra) with the same prompt. It costs about \$0.07 per thousand calls, so a
full comparison costs around ten cents.

Two models are compared by playing each other on a fixed set of 100 boards. Each
board is played twice with the sides swapped, so neither model benefits from
moving first or from getting an easy board. The boards use 150 words that no model
was trained on. Most comparisons are against a fixed opponent, the current
incumbent. For each pair we report the win rate, and as the main test a sign test
on the boards that one model won in both seatings.

Most of the models below have two parts. The first is a \emph{listener}, which
predicts what a guesser picks for a given clue. The second is a \emph{decision
rule}, which searches about 11,000 candidate clues using the listener and gives
the best one. The listener can be scored on its own, which we do with McFadden
$R^2$, that is, one minus the model's log-loss on the guess actually made divided
by the log-loss of guessing uniformly among the words left. This is measured on a
held-out set of 4,378 guesses by Claude Sonnet 5.5 on words that no model was
trained on. So $R^2$ measures how well a model understands the guesser, and win
rate measures how well the whole spymaster plays. As we will see, the two do not
always move together.

\section{The Gaussian model}

First we assume that the guesser perceives the similarity between the clue and
each board word as the similarity in an embedding space (ConceptNet Numberbatch,
standardized per word) plus independent Gaussian noise with standard deviation
$\sigma$, and that it picks words in decreasing order of the noisy similarity. An
own word is worth $+1$, and a neutral, opponent and assassin word cost $0.2$, $1$
and $10$. Under this assumption the expected reward of announcing $k$ has a closed
form, so we can score every clue and number in the pool exactly and give the best
one.

The only parameter is $\sigma$, which says how far the guesser's sense of
similarity is from the embedding's. We swept it against a simple centroid
baseline, with Sonnet guessing, on 50 boards each played both ways.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\toprule
$\sigma$ & win\% vs centroid & mean number & losses in the race & losses to the assassin \\
\midrule
1.0  & 69 & 2.67 & 17 & 14 \\
1.25 & 70 & 2.27 & 22 & 8 \\
1.5  & 70 & 1.93 & 24 & 6 \\
2.0  & 57 & 1.42 & 41 & 2 \\
2.5  & 51 & 1.16 & 46 & 3 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The win rate is flat between $\sigma = 1.0$ and $1.5$ and drops above that. Within
the flat part, a smaller $\sigma$ gives bigger numbers, which wins more races but
loses more games to the assassin, so we kept $\sigma = 1.5$, which has the same win
rate as $1.0$ with less than half the assassin losses. We also fit $\sigma$
directly to how Sonnet ranks words and got about $2.06$, which is in the region
that plays worse. So the $\sigma$ that best describes the guesser is not the one
that plays best. A spymaster that thinks the guesser is a bit sharper than it is
gives bigger clues, and the extra tempo wins races that the per-turn reward does
not account for. This comes up again later: fitting the guesser better and
winning more games are related but not the same thing.

\section{A learned listener (GBT)}

The Gaussian model describes the guesser with one noise parameter on one
embedding. The next step was to learn the guesser instead. We bought gpt-oss's
full rankings for about 25,000 positions (47,223 individual picks), many of them on
deliberately bad clues, so that the model also sees what a guesser does when a
clue does not work. A LightGBM model scores each (clue, board word) pair, and the
probability that a word is picked next is a softmax over the scores of the words
still on the board, which is also the training objective. The final version has
44 features per pair: similarities in several embedding spaces, human
free-association norms in both directions (SWOW), language-model pointwise mutual
information, concreteness norms and WordNet relations. Most of the gain over
embeddings alone comes from the association and PMI features.

If we treat the scores as a Plackett--Luce model, the expected reward of a clue
again has an exact closed form. The search takes the Gaussian model's top 200
clues and rescores them with the listener.

As a model of the guesser, this listener has held-out $R^2$ 0.346 against the
Gaussian model's 0.226 on gpt-oss's rankings of held-out boards. In games with
Sonnet guessing, it beat the Gaussian model at $\sigma = 1.5$ in 72\% of 100 games
(95\% CI 63--80\%). We also played it against Claude Sonnet 5 as the spymaster, with
Sonnet guessing for both sides, and it won 58\% of 200 games (28 boards won both
ways against 12, $p = 0.017$). Most of that margin came from the assassin, since
Sonnet's clues led its own guesser to the assassin 31 times against 13. This model
(\texttt{learned\_listener}) became the incumbent and the fixed opponent for
everything below. Its $R^2$ on the Sonnet 5.5 held-out set is 0.3205, which is the
baseline in the table of Section~\ref{sec:table}.

\section{Association features from gpt-oss}

Embeddings capture how words are used, but what a guesser picks depends on what
the clue reminds them of. So we asked gpt-oss for that directly, with no board:
``List the 25 words that the word \emph{clue} most strongly makes you think of.''
We asked five times per word at temperature 1, for every clue in the pool and every
board word. From these lists we get two features per (clue, board word) pair: the
share of lists that name the board word, and a reciprocal-rank score, so that a
word named first counts much more than one named 25th. For example, every list for
\clue{river}{} names \emph{water} first, and \emph{bank} and \emph{mouth} appear in
their river senses. A second version, the association profile, adds features of
the clue itself: how much its five lists agree (how vague it is), its rarity and
number of senses, and whether each board word's own lists name the clue.

These lists cost nothing at play time, since every clue in the pool already has
them. Since they come from the same model that guesses in the arena, there is a
risk that they only help predict gpt-oss, which is why $R^2$ is measured on
Sonnet's guesses, and Sonnet never saw these lists. $R^2$ goes from 0.3240 to 0.3293
with the associations and to 0.3309 with the profile, the largest single feature
gain on Sonnet. In games, the association listener alone is not significant
against the incumbent (53.1\%, $p = 0.43$). The profile version wins 56.7\% (22
boards won both ways against 9, $p = 0.029$), and it became the new incumbent.

\section{Deciding by win probability}

So far the decision rule maximizes the expected net words of the turn. This does
not account for tempo, since two words are worth more when they leave us one word
from winning. So we estimated $V(a, b)$, the probability that the side to move
wins with $a$ words left against $b$, from 10,266 finished gpt-oss games, smoothed
and made monotone. The rule then gives the clue with the highest probability of
winning after the turn. For each way the turn can end, it multiplies the
listener's probability of that ending by $V$ of the position it leaves, and sums.

With the incumbent's listener, this rule is level with the old one (51.5\%). With
the association listener it gives the best result we have on this suite, 59.1\%
(28 boards against 10, $p = 0.005$). The new rule uses the listener's probabilities
directly, rather than mostly to rank clues, so a better listener matters more
under it. The cost is about twice the incumbent's assassin losses. This model gives
bigger numbers (2.6 on average against 2.3), and some of those bets fail badly.

\section{Later picks: temperatures and pick order}

The listener gives each word one score per clue, and every pick of the turn uses
the same scores. But gpt-oss is much less predictable on its later picks: the
incumbent's $R^2$ is 0.55 on the first pick and 0.06 on the fourth. Using the same
confidence for pick 3 as for pick 1 makes the spymaster overrate clues for 3 or 4.

The first fix is per-pick temperatures, where we divide the scores at pick $j$ by
a fitted $\tau_j$, with $\tau_1 = 1$. The fitted values grow with the pick (1.09, 1.26,
1.41) and are nearly the same for every listener, so the flattening is a property
of the guesser and not of one model. $R^2$ goes from 0.3205 to 0.3271 on the
incumbent's listener and from 0.3309 to 0.3372 on the profile listener, all of it in
the later picks. In games, however, the temperature version of the incumbent lost
44.3\% (8 boards against 19, $p = 0.05$). The better fit made it more cautious, and
caution lost races, which is the same thing the $\sigma$ sweep showed.

The second fix is to let the model learn what changes from pick to pick. We add
two features, the pick index $j$ and $j - k$ (how far past the announced number the
pick is), and each pick is still a softmax over the words left. This fits slightly
better than the temperatures (0.3331 against 0.3295 on the 44 features, and 0.3374
on the association features) and leaves the first pick unchanged, as it should.
Adding which words were already picked does better still ($R^2$ 0.357), but it
needs about 130 model evaluations per clue, which is too slow to play. In games, the
pick-order listener with the win-probability rule and a one-move lookahead at the
opponent's reply wins 57.6\% against the incumbent (27 boards against 13, $p =
0.038$). Against the same spymaster without the pick-order features, though, the
difference is $+2.4$ points with a 95\% interval of $[-4.7, +9.4]$, so we cannot tell
them apart.

\section{Looking at the clues, and the next step}

All of the above optimizes win rate against gpt-oss, and it went up. But when we
played with the best models ourselves and looked at their clues, many of them made
no sense to a person. For example, near the start of a game the pick-order model
gave \clue{obscure}{4} for \emph{Alien}, \emph{Triangle}, \emph{Spot} and
\emph{Sound}. No person gets \emph{Triangle} or \emph{Sound} from that.

We think there are a few reasons for this. First, the models maximize expected
value, while people playing Codenames tend to give the clue that does best in the
outcome they think is most likely. Expected value is probably the more correct
thing to maximize, and it will sometimes give clues where the guesser is unsure
which word goes with it but which are good on average. We do not necessarily want
to prevent that, even if it makes the spymaster play unlike a human. Second, the
win-probability rule chases variance when behind. After the opponent finds 3 words
on the first clue, it thinks it has to catch up with a high-variance clue, which is
usually not true that early. Third, the training data never says no. The listener
was trained on gpt-oss ranking every word on the board, and a ranking always has a
3rd and 4th word, so the model learns how gpt-oss orders words it has no real
reason to pick, and a clue for 4 looks like it buys extra guesses nearly for free.
People do not rank 25 words. They see a clue as pointing at a word or not, and they
stop when nothing is left. So we were partly optimizing $R^2$ on later picks that
only reflect gpt-oss's habits.

So the next step is a measure closer to how a model would do with people, which we
call comfort: how clear it is which words a clue is meant for. Win rate stays, and
comfort is a second axis next to it.

First we changed how the guesser is asked, into two separate calls. The first call
shows the board and the clue without the number, and asks which words the clue
clearly points at (``guess''), which it would still take as a second or third word
if asked for more (``stretch''), and which it would not guess. The answer is a set
in board order with no ranking. The second call ranks only the words in that set,
now with the number. The guesser guesses only words in its set and stops when they
run out, so it can stop early or pass.

The same set labels give a new way to train the listener. At each pick it chooses
among the words left and STOP, so it predicts when a guesser would stop instead of
assuming it always uses the full number. On top of this listener we added an
overpromise penalty, a cost of 0.5 or 1 word for each promised word the guesser
does not attempt, with the number chosen by the objective.

To measure comfort, we take for every clue the set of words gpt-oss says it clearly
points at, which the first call already gives, and compute $\min(\text{set size},
N)/N$. A score of 1 means the clear words always covered the number. As a check we
asked gpt-oss separately which words it was sure the clue was meant for, on 100
clues per model. 93\% of those words were the team's, and the two measures rank the
models almost identically (Spearman 0.97).

We then reran the current models with the two-step guesser, against the
association-profile incumbent.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
model & win\% & comfort & mean number \\
\midrule
win probability, association listener & 54.5 & 0.756 & 2.62 \\
win probability + overpromise 1.0 & 53.5 & 0.873 & 2.14 \\
overpromise 0.5, strict STOP listener & 52.0 & 0.929 & 1.84 \\
association profile (incumbent) & 50.5$^*$ & 0.837 & 2.20 \\
overpromise 1.0, strict STOP listener & 45.5 & 0.944 & 1.73 \\
STOP listener, loose & 49.0 & 0.781 & 2.50 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
{\small $^*$ The incumbent's average over all its pairings.}

No model beats the incumbent on wins by a significant margin under the new
guesser, but the differences in comfort are large and clear. The overpromise model
at 0.5 is more comfortable than the incumbent ($t = -8.2$ on the per-board rate of
stopping early) and gives up nothing measurable in wins. Combining the
win-probability rule with the overpromise penalty at 1.0 lands between the two,
which is about what a trade-off between them would give, but not better than it.
The STOP listener trained on the looser set lost to the assassin 19 times against
4 ($p = 0.002$), since it expected the guesser to keep going onto weak words, and
this guesser does not.

The next steps are as follows. The penalty in the combined model is converted into
win probability using the value of one word at the current score, and that value is
zero at the opening because $V$ is flat there, so the penalty does nothing on the
first clue. We need to smooth it. After that we want a clarity setting, where among
clues within $\varepsilon$ of the best win probability we give the clearest one, and
sweep $\varepsilon$. Finally we need to check the comfortable models against real
people, since that is the point of all of this.
