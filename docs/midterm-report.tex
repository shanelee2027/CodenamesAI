\section{Setup}

Our goal is to create a model that gives Codenames clues meant for human
guessers.

One clear way to determine which models are better than others is to simulate
games of Codenames and record which models have the highest win rates. However,
it is impractical to have people play hundreds of games for every model, so we
let the guesser be a modern language model. The hope is that language models
think similarly to humans in terms of word association and game strategy while
being much cheaper to generate data with.

Here is the prompt that we give the language model:

\begin{quote}\small
You are playing the guesser role in the board game Codenames. Your spymaster gave
the clue ``\emph{clue}'' for \emph{N} word(s). Here are the words still available to
guess: \emph{[words]}. Rank ALL of the words above by how likely you would guess
them for this clue, most likely first. Respond with ONLY a JSON array of the words
[\ldots]
\end{quote}

We assume that the guesser guesses the first $N$ words in its list in order. So
under this prompt the guesser always takes exactly $N$ guesses unless it hits a
wrong word first.

The original plan was to use Claude Sonnet 5 (later 5.5) as the guesser, and we
still plan to use it for final evaluations. However, to save money on API
calls, we chose to use gpt-oss-120b (open weights, served by DeepInfra) for most
games, as it is much cheaper (about \$0.07 per thousand calls) and lets us
generate a lot of training data for our models.

Two spymaster models are compared by playing each other on a fixed set of 100
boards. Each board is played twice with the sides swapped, so neither model
benefits from moving first or from getting an easy board. Although Codenames
uses a base set of 400 words, we build evaluation boards from a subset of 150
words that no model was trained on, to help prevent overfitting. For each pair,
we report the win rate, and as the main test a sign test on the boards that one
model won in both seatings.

Most comparisons are against a fixed opponent, the \emph{incumbent}, which is
the best model so far. When a new model beats the incumbent significantly, it
becomes the new incumbent and later models are compared against it. In order,
the incumbents were the centroid model, the Gaussian model, the GBT listener and
the association-profile listener, all described below.

Most of the models below have two parts. The first is a \emph{listener}, which
predicts what a guesser picks for a given clue. The second is a \emph{decision
rule}, which searches about 11,000 candidate clues using the listener and gives
the best one. The listener can be scored on its own, which we do with McFadden
$R^2$. If at guess $i$ the guesser picked word $w_i$ out of the $n_i$ words left,
and the listener gave that word probability $\hat p(w_i)$, then
\[
R^2 \;=\; 1 - \frac{\sum_i \log \hat p(w_i)}{\sum_i \log (1/n_i)},
\]
that is, one minus the listener's log-loss divided by the log-loss of guessing
uniformly among the words left. So $R^2 = 0$ is no better than uniform and
$R^2 = 1$ predicts every guess with certainty. This is measured on a held-out
set of 4,378 guesses by Claude Sonnet 5.5 on boards made from the same 150
held-out words. Since most of our training data and most of our games use
gpt-oss, scoring on Sonnet also checks that the listeners are not just learning
gpt-oss's habits. So $R^2$ measures how well a model understands the guesser,
and win rate measures how well the whole spymaster plays. As we will see, the
two do not always move together.

\section{The Gaussian model}

First we assume that the guesser perceives the similarity between the clue and
each board word as the similarity in an embedding space (ConceptNet Numberbatch,
standardized per word) plus independent Gaussian noise with standard deviation
$\sigma$, and that it picks words in decreasing order of the noisy similarity. We
also assign rewards to the words the guesser picks: an own word is worth $+1$,
and a neutral, opponent and assassin word cost $0.2$, $1$ and $10$. These values
were chosen somewhat arbitrarily, though a later sweep over 15 alternative values
(changing one cost at a time) found none that did significantly better. Under
this assumption the expected reward of announcing $k$ has a closed form, so we
can score every clue and number in the pool exactly and give the best one.

The only parameter is $\sigma$, which says how far the guesser's sense of
similarity is from the embedding's. We swept it against the first incumbent, a
centroid model. The centroid model picks a random subset of one to four of its
team's words, gives the clue with the highest average similarity to them, and
announces how many of its team's words come before the first other word when the
board is sorted by similarity to the clue. Each $\sigma$ played 50 boards both
ways (100 games) with Sonnet guessing. The mean number is the average number
announced per clue, and the last two columns split the Gaussian model's losses
into games it lost by picking the assassin and games it lost because the
opponent found all its words first.

$\sigma$ & win\% & mean number & lost to the assassin (\%) & other losses (\%) \\
\midrule
1.0  & 69 & 2.67 & 14 & 17 \\
1.25 & 70 & 2.27 & 8  & 22 \\
1.5  & 70 & 1.93 & 6  & 24 \\
2.0  & 57 & 1.42 & 2  & 41 \\
2.5  & 51 & 1.16 & 3  & 46 \\
\bottomrule

The win rate is flat between $\sigma = 1.0$ and $1.5$ and drops above that. A
smaller $\sigma$ means the model believes the guesser is more accurate, so it
announces bigger numbers. It then loses more games to the assassin and fewer
games otherwise. We kept $\sigma = 1.5$, which has the same win rate as $1.0$
while losing less than half as many games to the assassin, and it became the
incumbent.

We also fit $\sigma$ directly to Sonnet's guesses, by maximum likelihood on the
first word of each of 3,403 rankings Sonnet gave in earlier games. Let $z_w$ be
the standardized similarity of board word $w$ to the clue, $S$ the words on the
board and $\phi$, $\Phi$ the standard normal density and CDF. Under the model, the
probability that word $a$ is picked first is
\[
P(a \mid \sigma) \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(u) \prod_{b \in S,\, b \neq a}
\Phi\!\left(u + \frac{z_a - z_b}{\sigma}\right) du,
\]
which is the probability that $a$'s noisy similarity beats every other word's,
conditioning on $a$'s own noise $\sigma u$. We maximize $\sum \log P(a \mid
\sigma)$ over the rankings. Only the first pick is used because under Gaussian
noise the later picks have no exact likelihood of this form. The fit gives
$\sigma = 2.06$ (95\% interval $[2.00, 2.11]$), which is in the region that plays
worse. So the $\sigma$ that best describes the guesser is not the one that plays
best. This comes up again later: fitting the guesser better and winning more
games are related but not the same thing.

\section{A learned listener (GBT)}

The Gaussian model describes the guesser with one noise parameter on one
embedding. The next step was to learn the guesser instead. We bought gpt-oss's
full rankings for about 25,000 positions (47,223 individual picks), many of them
on deliberately bad clues, so that the model also sees what a guesser does when a
clue does not work. A LightGBM model gives each (clue, board word) pair a score
$s_w$. The final version has 44 features per pair: similarities in several
embedding spaces, human free-association norms in both directions (SWOW),
language-model pointwise mutual information, concreteness norms and WordNet
relations. Most of the gain over embeddings alone comes from the association and
PMI features.

The scores are turned into guesses with a Plackett--Luce model: the guesser picks
words one at a time, each from a softmax over the words not yet picked. If $S_j$
is the set of words left before the $j$-th pick, the probability of the picks
$w_1, \ldots, w_m$ is
\[
P(w_1, \ldots, w_m) \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{m} \frac{e^{s_{w_j}}}{\sum_{w \in S_j} e^{s_w}}.
\]
The model is trained by maximizing this likelihood on gpt-oss's rankings. Under
it, the expected reward of a clue again has an exact closed form, without summing
over every possible order of picks, because these picks have the same
distribution as the order in which independent exponential clocks with rates
$e^{s_w}$ ring. The search takes the Gaussian model's top 200 clues and rescores
them with the listener.

On a fresh test set of gpt-oss rankings, this listener has $R^2$ 0.346 against
the Gaussian model's 0.226. In games with Sonnet guessing and the same rewards
as the Gaussian model, it beat the Gaussian model at $\sigma = 1.5$ in 72\% of
100 games (95\% CI 63--80\%). We also played it against Claude Sonnet 5 as the
spymaster, with Sonnet guessing for both sides, and it won 58\% of 200 games (28
boards won both ways against 12, $p = 0.017$). So against Sonnet as the guesser
it gives better clues than Sonnet does, though most of the margin came from the
assassin: Sonnet's clues led its own guesser to the assassin 31 times against 13.
This model (\texttt{learned\_listener}) became the incumbent. Its $R^2$ on the
Sonnet 5.5 held-out set is 0.3205, which is the baseline in the table of
Section~\ref{sec:table}.

\section{Association features from gpt-oss}

Embeddings capture how words are used, but what a guesser picks depends on what
the clue reminds them of. So we asked gpt-oss for that directly, with no board:
``List the 25 words that the word \emph{clue} most strongly makes you think of.''
We asked five times per word at temperature 1, for every clue in the pool and
every board word. For example, every list for \clue{river}{} names \emph{water}
first, and \emph{bank} and \emph{mouth} appear in their river senses. From these
lists we add features of each (clue, board word) pair: the share of the clue's
lists that name the board word, a reciprocal-rank score so that a word named
first counts much more than one named 25th, and whether the board word's own
lists name the clue. We also add features of the clue itself: how much its five
lists agree (how vague it is), its rarity and its number of senses.

Although these features are generated from gpt-oss, they help in predicting
Sonnet's guesses, improving $R^2$ from 0.3205 to 0.3309, the largest gain from
any set of features we added. In games against the GBT listener, the
association-profile listener wins 56.7\% (22 boards won both ways against 9,
$p = 0.029$), and it became the new incumbent.

\section{Deciding by win probability}

So far the decision rule maximizes the reward, where the rewards per word were
chosen somewhat arbitrarily to be $+1$, $-0.2$, $-1$ and $-10$. However, in
Codenames and many similar games, a team's best move depends on whether it is
ahead or behind. If, for example, a team has 3 words left to get while the other
has 1, this team should in practice give a clue for 3 words even if that is
suboptimal under our reward function, since the other team will most likely
win on its next turn.

So we estimated $V(a, b)$, the probability that the side to move wins with $a$
words left against $b$, from 10,266 finished gpt-oss games, smoothed and made
monotone. The rule then gives the clue with the highest probability of winning
after the turn. For each way the turn can end, it multiplies the listener's
probability of that ending by $V$ of the position it leaves, and sums.

With the GBT listener, this rule is level with the old one (51.5\%). With the
association-profile listener, it wins 57.2\% against the GBT listener (20 boards
won both ways against 7, $p = 0.019$, on 90 boards; on the other 10 gpt-oss would
not rank the words for one of the clues). The new rule uses the listener's
probabilities directly, rather than mostly to rank clues, so a better listener
matters more under it. The cost is about twice the GBT listener's assassin losses
(18 games against 9). It did not replace the incumbent, since when we later
played it against the association-profile model directly (Section~\ref{sec:next})
it was not significantly ahead.

\section{Later picks: temperatures and pick order}

The listener gives each word one score per clue, and every pick of the turn uses
the same scores. But gpt-oss is much less predictable on its later picks: the
GBT listener's $R^2$ is 0.55 on the first pick and 0.06 on the fourth. Using the
same confidence for pick 3 as for pick 1 makes the spymaster overrate clues for 3
or 4.

The first fix is per-pick temperatures, where we divide the scores at pick $j$ by
a fitted $\tau_j$, with $\tau_1 = 1$. The fitted values grow with the pick (1.09,
1.26, 1.41) and are nearly the same for every listener, so the flattening is a
property of the guesser and not of one model. $R^2$ goes from 0.3205 to 0.3271
on the GBT listener and from 0.3309 to 0.3372 on the association-profile
listener, all of it in the later picks. In games, however, the GBT listener with
temperatures lost to the GBT listener without them, 44.3\% (8 boards against 19,
$p = 0.05$).

The second fix is to let the model learn what changes from pick to pick. We add
two features, the pick index $j$ and $j - k$ (how far past the announced number
the pick is), and each pick is still a softmax over the words left. On the 44
features this fits slightly better than the temperatures ($R^2$ 0.3331 against
0.3295) and leaves the first pick unchanged, as it should. Adding which words
were already picked does better still ($R^2$ 0.357), but it needs about 130 model
evaluations per clue, which is too slow to play. In games, the pick-order
listener\footnote{This model and the win-probability rows in
Section~\ref{sec:next} use the gpt-oss association lists without the clue-profile
features.} with the win-probability rule and a one-move lookahead at the
opponent's reply wins 57.6\% against the GBT listener (27 boards against 13, $p =
0.038$). Against the same spymaster without the pick-order features, though, the
difference is $+2.4$ points with a 95\% interval of $[-4.7, +9.4]$, so we cannot
tell them apart.

\section{Looking at the clues, and the next step}
\label{sec:next}

All of the above optimizes win rate against gpt-oss, and it went up. But when we
played with the best models ourselves and looked at their clues, many of them made
no sense to a person. For example, near the start of a game the pick-order model
gave \clue{obscure}{4} for \emph{Alien}, \emph{Triangle}, \emph{Spot} and
\emph{Sound}. No person gets \emph{Triangle} or \emph{Sound} from that.

We think there are a few reasons for this. First, the models maximize expected
value, while people playing Codenames tend to give the clue that does best in the
outcome they think is most likely. Expected value is probably the more correct
thing to maximize, and it will sometimes give clues where the guesser is unsure
which word goes with it but which are good on average. We do not necessarily want
to prevent that, even if it makes the spymaster play unlike a human. Second, the
win-probability rule chases variance when behind. After the opponent finds 3 words
on the first clue, it thinks it has to catch up with a high-variance clue, which is
usually not true that early. Third, the training data never says no. The listener
was trained on gpt-oss ranking every word on the board, and a ranking always has a
3rd and 4th word, so the model learns how gpt-oss orders words it has no real
reason to pick, and a clue for 4 looks like it buys extra guesses nearly for free.
People do not rank 25 words. They see a clue as pointing at a word or not, and they
stop when nothing is left. So we were partly optimizing $R^2$ on later picks that
only reflect gpt-oss's habits.

So the next step is a measure closer to how a model would do with people, which we
call comfort: how clear it is which words a clue is meant for. Win rate stays, and
comfort is a second axis next to it.

First we changed how the guesser is asked, into two separate calls. The first call
shows the board and the clue without the number, and asks which words the clue
clearly points at (``guess''), which it would still take as a second or third word
if asked for more (``stretch''), and which it would not guess. The answer is a set
in board order with no ranking. The second call ranks only the words in that set,
now with the number. The guesser guesses only words in its set and stops when they
run out, so it can stop early or pass.

The same set labels give a new way to train the listener. At each pick it chooses
among the words left and STOP, so it predicts when a guesser would stop instead of
assuming it always uses the full number. On top of this listener we added an
overpromise penalty, a cost of 0.5 or 1 word for each promised word the guesser
does not attempt, with the number chosen by the objective.

To measure comfort, we take for every clue the set of words gpt-oss says it clearly
points at, which the first call already gives, and compute $\min(\text{set size},
N)/N$. A score of 1 means the clear words always covered the number. As a check we
asked gpt-oss separately which words it was sure the clue was meant for, on 100
clues per model. 93\% of those words were the team's, and the two measures rank the
models almost identically (Spearman 0.97).

We then reran the current models with the two-step guesser, against the
association-profile incumbent.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
model & win\% & comfort & mean number \\
\midrule
win probability & 54.5 & 0.756 & 2.62 \\
win probability + overpromise 1.0 & 53.5 & 0.873 & 2.14 \\
overpromise 0.5, strict STOP listener & 52.0 & 0.929 & 1.84 \\
association profile (incumbent) & 50.5$^*$ & 0.837 & 2.20 \\
overpromise 1.0, strict STOP listener & 45.5 & 0.944 & 1.73 \\
STOP listener, loose & 49.0 & 0.781 & 2.50 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
{\small $^*$ The incumbent's average over all its pairings.}

No model beats the incumbent on wins by a significant margin under the new
guesser (the win-probability model's 54.5\% has sign $p = 0.19$), but the
differences in comfort are large and clear. The overpromise model at 0.5 is more
comfortable than the incumbent ($t = -8.2$ on the per-board rate of stopping
early) and gives up nothing measurable in wins. Combining the win-probability
rule with the overpromise penalty at 1.0 lands between the two, which is about
what a trade-off between them would give, but not better than it. The STOP
listener trained on the looser set lost to the assassin 19 times against 4 ($p =
0.002$), since it expected the guesser to keep going onto weak words, and this
guesser does not.

The next steps are as follows. The penalty in the combined model is converted into
win probability using the value of one word at the current score, and that value is
zero at the opening because $V$ is flat there, so the penalty does nothing on the
first clue. We need to smooth it. After that we want a clarity setting, where among
clues within $\varepsilon$ of the best win probability we give the clearest one, and
sweep $\varepsilon$. Finally we need to check the comfortable models against real
people, since that is the point of all of this.
