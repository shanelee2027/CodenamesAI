Clue Selection Under Noise

Codenames spymaster, baseline model

1Setup

Let \(A\) be the set of board words we want the guesser to select and \(B\) the set we want them to avoid. A clue is a clue word together with a number \(k\), and announcing it allows the guesser \(k\) selections. Fix a clue word, and let \(a_i\) be its similarity to word \(i \in A\) and \(b_w\) its similarity to word \(w \in B\), where the \(a_i\) are sorted so that \(a_1 \ge a_2 \ge \dots\). Selecting a word of \(A\) is worth \(1\), and selecting \(w \in B\) costs \(c_w > 0\). We want to choose both parts of the clue.

We assume the guesser perceives word \(x\)'s similarity with the clue word as \(\hat z_x = z_x + \varepsilon_x\), where \(z_x\) is our similarity to \(x\) and the \(\varepsilon_x\) are independent \(N(0, \sigma^2)\). This models differences between our interpreted embeddings and the guesser's - higher \(\sigma\) means a larger gap between the two. We also assume the guesser selects unrevealed words in decreasing order of \(\hat z\), stopping after \(k\) selections or upon selecting a word of \(B\), whichever comes first.

In words, the guesser ranks the board the same way we do, perturbed by independent noise of a single scale, and then guesses greedily. \(\sigma\) is the only free parameter.

2Reduction

Let

\[ D = \max_{w \in B} \hat z_w, \qquad W^\ast = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{w \in B} \hat z_w, \qquad N = \#\{\, i \in A : \hat z_i > D \,\}. \]

Every word ranked above \(D\) is in \(A\), since \(D\) is the maximum of \(\hat z\) over \(B\). Thus the guesser's ordering begins with the \(N\) words counted above, and the \((N+1)\)-th word is \(W^\ast\). This means the guesser reveals \(\min(k, N)\) words of \(A\), and selects a word of \(B\) if and only if \(N < k\), in which case that word is \(W^\ast\).

Note that no word of \(B\) other than \(W^\ast\) can be selected, so the single random variable \(D\) accounts for all of \(B\). Write \(\varphi\) and \(\Phi\) for the density and the distribution function of a standard normal. Each \(\hat z_w\) is \(N(b_w, \sigma^2)\), so

\[ F_{\hat z_w}(x) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x - b_w}{\sigma}\right), \qquad f_{\hat z_w}(x) = \sigma^{-1}\varphi\!\left(\frac{x - b_w}{\sigma}\right). \]

Since \(D\) is a maximum of independent variables,

\[ F_D(d) \;=\; \Pr(D \le d) \;=\; \prod_{w \in B} \Phi\!\left(\frac{d - b_w}{\sigma}\right). \]

3Conditional distribution

\(D\) is a function of \((\varepsilon_w)_{w \in B}\) only, which is independent of \((\varepsilon_i)_{i \in A}\). Thus conditioning on \(D = d\) does not change the distribution of the \(\hat z_i\), and each \(i \in A\) independently satisfies \(\hat z_i > d\) with probability

\[ p_i(d) \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{a_i - d}{\sigma}\right). \]

Given \(D = d\), the count \(N\) is therefore a sum of \(|A|\) independent Bernoulli variables, the \(i\)-th with success probability \(p_i(d)\).

4Objective

From Section 2 the guesser reveals \(\min(k, N)\) words of \(A\), and selects \(W^\ast\) exactly when \(N < k\). Each word of \(A\) is worth \(1\) and \(W^\ast\) costs \(c_{W^\ast}\), so the value of announcing \(k\) is \(G(k) - P(k)\), where

\[ G(k) = \mathbb E\bigl[\min(k, N)\bigr], \qquad P(k) = \mathbb E\bigl[\, c_{W^\ast}\,\mathbf 1\{N < k\} \,\bigr]. \]

Since \(\min(k, N) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \mathbf 1\{N \ge j\}\), taking expectations and conditioning on \(D\) gives

\[ G(k) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{k} \int \Pr(N \ge j \mid D = d) \,\, dF_D(d). \]

Each \(\Pr(N \ge j \mid D = d)\) follows from the Bernoulli sum of Section 3, and the integral is one-dimensional in \(d\). Section 6 computes both. It remains to find \(P\).

In \(P\) the cost \(c_{W^\ast}\) and the event \(\{N < k\}\) both depend on the noise, but they become independent once \(D\) is fixed. To see this, note that \(D\) is a function of \((\varepsilon_w)_{w \in B}\) alone, so conditioning on \(D = d\) constrains that family and leaves \((\varepsilon_i)_{i \in A}\) with its original distribution. Given \(D = d\), the count \(N = \#\{i \in A : z_i + \varepsilon_i > d\}\) is a function of \((\varepsilon_i)_{i \in A}\), while the identity \(W^\ast\) is a function of \((\varepsilon_w)_{w \in B}\). The two families are independent, so \(N\) and \(W^\ast\) are conditionally independent given \(D\), and the expectation splits:

\[ P(k) \;=\; \int \Pr(N < k \mid D = d)\; \bar c(d) \,\, dF_D(d), \qquad \bar c(d) = \mathbb E\bigl[\, c_{W^\ast} \mid D = d \,\bigr]. \]

The first factor is again a probability for the Bernoulli sum of Section 3. It remains to compute \(\bar c(d)\), for which we need \(\Pr(W^\ast = w \mid D = d)\).

The event that \(W^\ast = w\) with \(D\) near \(d\) is the event that \(\hat z_w\) lies near \(d\) and every other \(\hat z_v\) is below \(d\). The \(\hat z\) are independent, so its density is the density of \(\hat z_w\) at \(d\) times the probability that each other \(\hat z_v\) falls below \(d\). Both were given in Section 2, so this density is

\[ \sigma^{-1}\varphi\!\left(\frac{d - b_w}{\sigma}\right) \prod_{v \ne w} \Phi\!\left(\frac{d - b_v}{\sigma}\right). \]

The product over \(v \ne w\) is \(F_D(d)\) with the \(w\)-th factor removed, so it equals \(F_D(d) \big/ \Phi\bigl((d - b_w)/\sigma\bigr)\). Set

\[ h_w(d) \;=\; \frac{\varphi\bigl((d - b_w)/\sigma\bigr)}{\Phi\bigl((d - b_w)/\sigma\bigr)}, \]

so the density above is \(\sigma^{-1} F_D(d)\, h_w(d)\). The events \(\{W^\ast = w\}\) are disjoint and cover everything, so summing over \(w\) gives the density of \(D\) at \(d\),

\[ f_D(d) \;=\; \sigma^{-1} F_D(d) \sum_v h_v(d). \]

Dividing the joint density by \(f_D(d)\), the factors \(\sigma^{-1}\) and \(F_D(d)\) cancel:

\[ \Pr(W^\ast = w \mid D = d) \;=\; \frac{h_w(d)}{\sum_v h_v(d)}, \qquad \bar c(d) \;=\; \frac{\sum_w c_w\, h_w(d)}{\sum_w h_w(d)}. \]

Thus \(\bar c(d)\) is a weighted average of the costs. The weight \(h_w(d)\) is large when \(b_w\) is near \(d\) and small when \(b_w\) lies well below it, so the average is over the words of \(B\) that can be the one selected. Note that the costs are averaged and not summed: only one word of \(B\) is ever selected, however many of them outrank \(a_k\).

We choose the clue maximizing the value,

\[ \text{clue}^\ast \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\text{word},\; 1 \le k \le K} \bigl[\, G(k) - P(k) \,\bigr]. \]

5Properties

From the sum defining \(G\),

\[ G(k) - G(k-1) \;=\; \Pr(N \ge k), \]

which is non-increasing in \(k\). Thus \(G\) is concave, and the marginal value of claiming one more word is exactly the probability of reaching it. Note that replacing \(G(k)\) with \(k\) gives an objective whose maximizer is \(k = K\) for every board, since the first term then grows by 1 for each additional word while \(P\) is bounded.

If \(b_w \to -\infty\) with all else fixed, then \(h_w \to 0\) and \(\Phi\bigl((d - b_w)/\sigma\bigr) \to 1\) wherever \(F_D\) has mass, so both \(G\) and \(P\) converge to their values with \(w\) removed from \(B\). Thus a sufficiently dissimilar word does not affect the objective, and only the weakest word of \(A\) being claimed and the strongest word of \(B\) carry weight.

The costs \(c_w\) enter only through \(\bar c\), and not through \(G\). Thus pricing one kind of mistake differently from another requires only changing the constants.

6Computation

Both integrals are one-dimensional and require no density. Partition the line into cells \([d_m, d_{m+1}]\). The mass of each cell is \(F_D(d_{m+1}) - F_D(d_m)\), which we have from Section 2, and we evaluate the conditional factors at the midpoint. The only error is discretization.

Given \(d\), the probabilities \(\Pr(N \ge j \mid D = d)\) follow from the standard recursion over \(A\). Letting \(q^{(m)}_r = \Pr(N = r)\) after the first \(m\) words of \(A\),

\[ q^{(0)}_0 = 1, \qquad q^{(m)}_r \;=\; q^{(m-1)}_r\bigl(1 - p_m(d)\bigr) \;+\; q^{(m-1)}_{r-1}\,p_m(d), \]

and \(\Pr(N \ge j \mid D = d) = 1 - \sum_{r < j} q_r\). Counts above \(k\) are never used, so the recursion may be truncated there, giving cost linear in \(|A|\) and in \(k\).

7Results

The model above has one free parameter. To choose it, \(\sigma\) was swept over three orders of magnitude and each value was made to play the same 100 positions, drawn from held-out board words and spanning openings through endgames. Each turn was scored as the game scores it: the guesser's ranking is walked, own words are counted until the first word of \(B\), and the reward is \(+1\) per own word with \(-0.2\), \(-1\) and \(-10\) for a neutral, opponent and assassin word respectively. The guesser was an external language model, not the similarity ranking the spymaster itself uses, so the numbers measure the model against a listener it has no access to.

Per turn, over 100 positions. announced is \(k\); revealed is the number of words of \(A\) the guesser actually took; the last four columns count how each turn ended.

Both limits behave as Section 5 requires. As \(\sigma \to 0\) the model treats the guesser as a copy of itself and announces \(k = K\) on nearly every board, and as \(\sigma\) grows it announces \(k = 1\) and stops responding to further increases. Neither end is optimal, and the reward curve is single-peaked between them.

Note that the number of words revealed and the reward earned are maximized at different \(\sigma\). Revealed words peak at \(\sigma = 1.0\) with \(1.74\) per turn, where reward is only \(0.852\); reward peaks at \(\sigma = 2.5\) with \(1.314\), where \(1.41\) words are revealed. The difference is entirely risk. At \(\sigma = 1.0\) the guesser reaches a word of \(B\) on 76 of 100 turns and the assassin on 4; at \(\sigma = 2.5\) it reaches \(B\) on 20 and the assassin on none. Thus the gain from claiming an extra word is real but is more than repaid, and an objective fitted on revealed words alone would choose a value that loses games.

The assassin is what makes the left half of the curve hard to measure. It is worth \(-10\), so a handful of assassin turns dominate the variance: the standard error is \(0.2\) to \(0.3\) for \(\sigma \le 2.0\) and \(0.035\) to \(0.069\) for \(\sigma \ge 2.5\), where no assassin was reached at all. Comparing paired by board, \(\sigma = 2.5\) beats every other value tested at the 5% level except \(\sigma = 2.0\) \((+0.14 \pm 0.21)\) and \(\sigma = 1.0\) \((+0.46 \pm 0.49)\), both of which are indistinguishable only because their own assassin losses are so noisy.

Note also that the gap between announced and revealed is negative everywhere and closes monotonically, reaching \(-0.11\) at \(\sigma = 4\). The model therefore remains optimistic at its own optimum, announcing \(1.72\) where \(1.41\) is delivered. Calibrating \(G\) and maximizing reward are different objectives: only the ordering of \(G(k) - P(k)\) across clues and \(k\) affects the choice made, so a bias shared by every candidate leaves the argmax unchanged. Fitting \(\sigma\) to close the gap would report \(\sigma \approx 4\) and cost \(0.25\) reward per turn.

We therefore take \(\sigma = 2.5\). The positions used here are disjoint from the evaluation boards, so the value was not chosen on the boards it is later reported on.

Here \(a_i\) and \(b_w\) are similarities standardized per clue, \(c_w\) are the costs of the words to avoid, and \(\sigma\) is the guesser's per-word perturbation.
