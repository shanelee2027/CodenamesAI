Clue Selection Under Noise

Codenames spymaster, baseline model

1Setup

Let \(A\) be the set of board words we want the guesser to select and \(B\) the set we want them to avoid. Fix a clue, and let \(a_i\) be its similarity to word \(i \in A\) and \(b_w\) its similarity to word \(w \in B\), where the \(a_i\) are sorted so that \(a_1 \ge a_2 \ge \dots\). Let \(c_w > 0\) be the cost incurred if the guesser selects \(w\). Announcing the number \(k\) allows the guesser \(k\) selections. We want to choose both the clue and \(k\).

We assume the guesser perceives word \(x\) as \(\hat z_x = z_x + \varepsilon_x\), where \(z_x\) is our similarity to \(x\) and the \(\varepsilon_x\) are independent \(N(0, \sigma^2)\). This models differences between our interpreted embeddings and the guesser's - higher \(\sigma\) means a larger gap between the two. We also assume the guesser selects unrevealed words in decreasing order of \(\hat z\), stopping after \(k\) selections or upon selecting a word of \(B\), whichever comes first.

In words, the guesser ranks the board the same way we do, perturbed by independent noise of a single scale, and then guesses greedily. \(\sigma\) is the only free parameter.

2Reduction

Let

\[ D = \max_{w \in B} \hat z_w, \qquad W^\ast = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{w \in B} \hat z_w, \qquad N = \#\{\, i \in A : \hat z_i > D \,\}. \]

Every word ranked above \(D\) is in \(A\), since \(D\) is the maximum of \(\hat z\) over \(B\). Thus the guesser's ordering begins with the \(N\) words counted above, and the \((N+1)\)-th word is \(W^\ast\). This means the guesser reveals \(\min(k, N)\) words of \(A\), and selects a word of \(B\) if and only if \(N < k\), in which case that word is \(W^\ast\).

Note that no word of \(B\) other than \(W^\ast\) can be selected, so the single random variable \(D\) accounts for all of \(B\). Since \(D\) is a maximum of independent normals,

\[ F(d) \;=\; \Pr(D \le d) \;=\; \prod_{w \in B} \Phi\!\left(\frac{d - b_w}{\sigma}\right). \]

3Conditional distribution

\(D\) is a function of \((\varepsilon_w)_{w \in B}\) only, which is independent of \((\varepsilon_i)_{i \in A}\). Thus conditioning on \(D = d\) does not change the law of the \(\hat z_i\), and each \(i \in A\) independently satisfies \(\hat z_i > d\) with probability

\[ p_i(d) \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{a_i - d}{\sigma}\right). \]

Given \(D = d\), the count \(N\) is therefore a sum of independent Bernoulli variables, that is, Poisson–binomial with parameters \(\{p_i(d)\}_{i \in A}\).

We condition on \(D\) rather than multiplying marginal probabilities because the events \(\{\hat z_i > \hat z_w\}\) are not independent. For fixed \(i\) they share \(\varepsilon_i\), and for fixed \(w\) they share \(\varepsilon_w\). Both dependencies are positive, so \(\prod_w \Phi\bigl((a_i - b_w)/\sigma\sqrt 2\bigr)\) is a strict lower bound on \(\Pr(\hat z_i > D)\) rather than an approximation to it. Conditioning on \(D\) removes both, since \(D\) is the only quantity the two groups share.

4Objective

Let \(G(k)\) be the expected number of words of \(A\) revealed and \(P(k)\) the expected cost incurred. From Section 2 the guesser reveals \(\min(k, N)\) words of \(A\), and \(\min(k, N) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \mathbf 1\{N \ge j\}\). Taking expectations and conditioning on \(D\),

\[ G(k) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{k} \int \Pr(N \ge j \mid D = d) \; dF(d). \]

A cost is incurred if and only if \(N < k\), and that cost is \(c_{W^\ast}\), so we need the law of \(W^\ast\) given \(D = d\). The density of \(\{W^\ast = w,\, D \in dd\}\) is

\[ \sigma^{-1}\varphi\!\left(\frac{d - b_w}{\sigma}\right) \prod_{v \ne w} \Phi\!\left(\frac{d - b_v}{\sigma}\right) \;=\; \sigma^{-1} F(d)\, h_w(d), \qquad h_w(d) = \frac{\varphi\bigl((d - b_w)/\sigma\bigr)}{\Phi\bigl((d - b_w)/\sigma\bigr)}. \]

Dividing by \(f(d) = \sigma^{-1} F(d) \sum_{v} h_v(d)\) gives \(\Pr(W^\ast = w \mid D = d) = h_w(d) / \sum_v h_v(d)\). Letting \(\bar c(d)\) be the expected cost under this law,

\[ \bar c(d) \;=\; \frac{\sum_w c_w\, h_w(d)}{\sum_w h_w(d)}, \qquad P(k) \;=\; \int \Pr(N < k \mid D = d)\; \bar c(d) \; dF(d). \]

Note that \(\bar c(d)\) is not the same as summing \(c_w\) over every \(w\) that might outrank \(a_k\), since only one word of \(B\) is ever selected. We then choose

\[ (\text{clue}^\ast, k^\ast) \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\text{clue},\; 1 \le k \le K} \bigl[\, G(k) - P(k) \,\bigr]. \]

5Properties

From the sum defining \(G\),

\[ G(k) - G(k-1) \;=\; \Pr(N \ge k), \]

which is non-increasing in \(k\). Thus \(G\) is concave, and the marginal value of claiming one more word is exactly the probability of reaching it. Note that replacing \(G(k)\) with \(k\) gives an objective whose maximizer is \(k = K\) for every board, since the first term then grows by 1 for each additional word while \(P\) is bounded.

If \(b_w \to -\infty\) with all else fixed, then \(h_w \to 0\) and \(\Phi\bigl((d - b_w)/\sigma\bigr) \to 1\) wherever \(F\) has mass, so both \(G\) and \(P\) converge to their values with \(w\) removed from \(B\). Thus a sufficiently dissimilar word does not affect the objective, and only the weakest word of \(A\) being claimed and the strongest word of \(B\) carry weight.

The costs \(c_w\) enter only through \(\bar c\), and not through \(G\). Thus pricing one kind of mistake differently from another requires only changing the constants.

6Computation

Both integrals are one-dimensional and require no density. Partition the line into cells \([d_m, d_{m+1}]\). The mass of each cell is \(F(d_{m+1}) - F(d_m)\), which we have from Section 2, and we evaluate the conditional factors at the midpoint. The only error is discretization.

Given \(d\), the Poisson–binomial tail follows from the standard recursion over \(A\). Letting \(q^{(m)}_r = \Pr(N = r)\) after the first \(m\) words of \(A\),

\[ q^{(0)}_0 = 1, \qquad q^{(m)}_r \;=\; q^{(m-1)}_r\bigl(1 - p_m(d)\bigr) \;+\; q^{(m-1)}_{r-1}\,p_m(d), \]

and \(\Pr(N \ge j \mid D = d) = 1 - \sum_{r < j} q_r\). Counts above \(k\) are never used, so the recursion may be truncated there, giving cost linear in \(|A|\) and in \(k\).

Here \(a_i\) and \(b_w\) are similarities standardized per clue, \(c_w\) are the costs of the words to avoid, and \(\sigma\) is the guesser's per-word perturbation.
